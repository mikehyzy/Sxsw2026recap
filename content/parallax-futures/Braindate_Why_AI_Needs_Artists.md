# Why Your AI Strategy Needs an Artist

## Session Metadata
- **Date:** Monday, March 16, 2026
- **Format:** Braindate (small-group, facilitated conversation)
- **Host:** Mike Hyzy, VP of AI Strategy, CGI / Parallax Futures
- **Participants:** ~8–10 professionals from diverse backgrounds including innovation labs (Intercorp, Peru), media archives (Fire Mountain), design and skincare technology, enterprise consulting (CGI, Toronto), and independent creative practice
- **Location:** SXSW Braindate Lounge

---

## Overview

This braindate explored a provocation at the heart of AI strategy: that the missing voice in most enterprise AI deployments isn't another engineer or product manager — it's an artist. The conversation moved fluidly between personal practice, organizational politics, and philosophical questions about what creativity means in an age of generative AI.

What made this session distinctive was the diversity of perspectives around the table. A business designer running innovation labs across Latin America, a media archivist digitizing decades of film and audio, a skincare technology professional, and strategy consultants all brought radically different lenses to the same question. The result was less a structured argument and more a live demonstration of exactly what the session title proposed: that putting creatively-trained minds in proximity to strategic problems produces insights that no single discipline generates alone.

---

## Key Themes

### Cognitive Enhancement vs. Cognitive Offloading

The central distinction Mike drew throughout the conversation was between using AI to enhance human cognition and using AI to replace it. Most organizations default to offloading — asking AI to write the memo, generate the strategy deck, produce the options. Enhancement looks different: using AI as a sparring partner, a challenger, a tool that forces sharper thinking rather than eliminating thinking entirely.

The practical test Mike offered: instead of asking AI to "write this for me," ask AI to "critique this." The shift from generation to critique keeps the human in the driver's seat — the person still has to produce the initial thinking, defend it, and decide what to do with the critique. This reframes AI from a replacement for thought to a catalyst for better thought.

> "The question isn't whether you use AI. It's whether AI is making you think harder or think less. If you're thinking less, you're not using it — it's using you."

### The Missing Creative Voice in the C-Suite

Multiple participants noted that creative roles — designers, artists, writers — are systematically excluded from strategic decision-making in most organizations. They're brought in to execute after decisions are made: design the interface, write the copy, make the deck look good. But the cognitive skills that creative training develops — comfort with ambiguity, lateral thinking, aesthetic judgment, the ability to hold contradictions — are precisely what AI strategy requires.

The business designer from Intercorp's innovation labs in Peru described running innovation programs across multiple industries (banking, retail, education) and consistently finding that the teams producing the most surprising solutions were the ones with at least one person trained in creative disciplines. Not because artists have better ideas, but because they ask different questions and tolerate longer periods of uncertainty before converging on solutions.

> "In our labs across Lima, Bogotá, Santiago — every time we put a designer or artist in a room with business people, the output changes. Not incrementally. Categorically."

### The Strategy Trifecta

Mike outlined his approach to strategy as a trifecta: business strategy, technology strategy, and creative strategy. Most organizations run on two of three at best. The argument isn't that creative strategy replaces the other two — it's that without it, the other two converge on the same solutions as every competitor using the same frameworks and the same AI tools.

This connects directly to the "super company" thesis from other SXSW sessions: if everyone's AI produces similar outputs, differentiation comes from the human inputs — and creative thinkers provide categorically different inputs than business or technology thinkers alone.

### Artists Must Evolve Too

The conversation wasn't one-sided advocacy for artists. Multiple participants — including Mike — pushed back on the idea that traditional artistic training automatically translates to business value. Artists who want a seat at the strategy table need to learn business language, understand commercial constraints, and demonstrate measurable impact.

The parallel drawn was to data science a decade ago: data scientists who could only build models in isolation had limited impact. The ones who transformed organizations were those who learned to communicate findings in business terms, connect analysis to decisions, and operate within organizational realities. Artists face the same evolution.

> "I'm not saying hire a painter and put them in your strategy meeting. I'm saying the cognitive toolkit that artistic training develops — that's what's missing. But the artist has to meet the business halfway."

### Real-World AI Applications: Digital Twins and Creative Tools

Mike shared his experience building a digital twin of himself on HeyGen — an AI-generated video avatar that can deliver presentations, respond to questions, and represent him in contexts where he can't physically be present. The point wasn't the technology itself but the creative decisions involved: what voice to use, what personality to project, how to maintain authenticity when the "you" on screen is algorithmically generated.

This became a case study in exactly the kind of problem that needs artistic judgment. The technical capability exists. The strategic rationale exists. But the decisions about tone, presence, authenticity, and uncanny valley — those are aesthetic and creative decisions that no framework answers.

### The Authenticity Hunger

Several participants observed that as AI-generated content floods every channel, audiences are developing an acute hunger for authenticity — for things that feel human-made, imperfect, and real. The media archivist from Fire Mountain described this from a unique angle: their work digitizing historical film and audio archives is becoming more valuable precisely because the material is irreproducibly authentic.

> "We're sitting on decades of original footage, original recordings. None of it was made by AI. And suddenly that's the most valuable thing about it — not what it contains, but that it's real."

This hunger for authenticity creates a strategic opportunity for organizations willing to invest in genuinely human creative work rather than AI-generated substitutes. The paradox: the more AI can produce, the more people value what it can't.

### Cultural Value vs. Financial Value

The conversation surfaced a persistent tension between cultural and financial value — and whether organizations can navigate both. The Intercorp innovation labs operate at this intersection, using design thinking and creative methods to generate commercial innovation while also building cultural capacity within organizations.

The skincare technology participant raised an adjacent point: in industries where the product is inherently personal and embodied (skincare, health, fashion), the cultural and aesthetic dimensions aren't nice-to-haves — they're core to the value proposition. AI can optimize formulations and supply chains, but the brand relationship, the sensory experience, and the emotional resonance require human creative judgment.

### The Role of Creatives in Strategy

The closing thread wove together the session's themes into a practical argument: creatives belong in strategy not as decorators but as cognitive diversifiers. Their value isn't in making things pretty — it's in making thinking different.

Specific roles discussed for creatives in AI strategy included scenario visualization (making abstract futures tangible and emotionally resonant), assumption challenging (creatives are trained to question what's taken for granted), experience design (ensuring AI deployments feel right to humans, not just work correctly), ethical provocation (artists ask "should we?" when engineers ask "can we?"), and narrative construction (framing AI initiatives in stories that organizations can rally around rather than in metrics alone).

## Participant Perspectives

**Business Designer (Intercorp Innovation Labs, Peru):** Brought a Latin American perspective on running innovation programs across banking, retail, and education sectors. Consistently observed that creative practitioners change not just outputs but the nature of inquiry within innovation teams. Working across Lima, Bogotá, and Santiago, they've built evidence that cross-disciplinary teams outperform homogeneous ones — and that the creative member is the most common missing piece.

**Media Archivist (Fire Mountain):** Described the unexpected renaissance in value of physical media archives — original film, audio recordings, photographs — as AI-generated content proliferates. Their work digitizing and preserving these archives positions them at a fascinating intersection: using AI tools (for cataloging, restoration, search) to preserve and surface inherently non-AI content. The authenticity premium they're seeing from clients and audiences validates the session's core thesis from a completely different angle.

**Design Professional (Toronto / CGI):** Contributed perspectives on integrating design thinking into enterprise consulting — the organizational resistance, the translation challenges, and the moments where creative approaches break through. Their experience at CGI mirrors the broader argument: large enterprise organizations have sophisticated business and technology capabilities but systematically underinvest in creative capacity.

**Skincare Technology Professional:** Offered a product-centered perspective on why aesthetic and sensory judgment can't be outsourced to AI. In industries where the product is experienced bodily — applied to skin, worn, tasted, lived in — the creative and cultural dimensions are inseparable from the functional ones.

**Independent Creative (Laura):** Grounded the conversation in the lived experience of a working creative navigating the AI landscape — the opportunities, the threats, the daily decisions about when to use AI tools and when to insist on human-only process.

## Takeaways for Practitioners

1. **Add a creative strategist to your AI initiative.** Not after decisions are made. During the decision-making process. The cognitive diversity pays for itself in differentiation.

2. **Shift your default AI prompt from "generate" to "critique."** This single behavioral change moves you from cognitive offloading to cognitive enhancement.

3. **Audit your strategy for the missing third leg.** If you have business strategy and technology strategy but no creative strategy, you're competing on the same terms as everyone else.

4. **Invest in authenticity as AI content scales.** Original human-made content, physical archives, and irreproducible creative work will become more valuable, not less, as AI generation becomes ubiquitous.

5. **Train artists in business; train businesspeople in art.** The translation layer between creative thinking and strategic decision-making is where the real value lives. Neither side can cross the gap alone.
