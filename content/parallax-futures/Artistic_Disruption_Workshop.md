## Overview

This workshop applied artistic disruption techniques — drawn from Surrealist and Situationist movements of the 1930s–1970s — to futures thinking and business innovation. Rather than lecturing about creativity, the session immersed participants in four escalating "provocations" that forced them to think differently, collaborate with strangers, and generate speculative futures through hands-on creative exercises.

The format was deliberately disorienting. Participants drew, wrote, passed materials to strangers, interpreted unfamiliar images, and co-authored visions of the future without the safety net of slide decks, frameworks, or best practices. The underlying premise: if your strategy process feels comfortable, it's probably not producing anything original. Artistic disruption methods — tested over nearly a century by avant-garde movements — can crack open the cognitive patterns that make business thinking predictable.

The workshop built from individual creative acts (Provocation #1) through collaborative interpretation (Provocation #2) to collective futures authoring (Provocation #3) and culminated in a group spectacle imagining SXSW in 2040 (Provocation #4). Each provocation increased the scale of collaboration and the time horizon of speculation.

## Workshop Structure: Four Provocations

### Provocation #1: One Into Another 2.0

<!-- IMAGE SUGGESTION: frames/canon/MVI_5629_frame_0001.jpg — Slide showing Provocation #1 structure with round details -->

Adapted from the Surrealist parlor game "One Into Another," this exercise forced participants through three rapid rounds of creative translation:

- **Round #1: Text** (~5 minutes) — Participants wrote a short text passage, then passed it to another table member who transformed it into something new
- **Round #2: Images** (~5 minutes) — The same pass-and-transform mechanic, now using visual imagery rather than words
- **Round #3: Mixed Media** (~5 minutes) — Combining text and images in a final round that blended both modes of expression

The original Surrealist game asked one player to secretly choose an object and describe it using the properties of a completely different object, forcing metaphorical leaps that bypassed rational thought. This updated version preserved that core mechanic — the deliberate collision of unrelated ideas to produce unexpected connections — while adapting it for group dynamics and business context.

<!-- IMAGE SUGGESTION: frames/canon/MVI_5629_frame_0007.jpg — "CHANGE PLACES" slide with participant artwork displayed on screen -->

Between rounds, the facilitator called "CHANGE PLACES" — a Situationist-inspired disruption where participants physically moved, breaking the social comfort that forms at tables and forcing new collaborations.

### Provocation #2: En'lan 2000

<!-- IMAGE SUGGESTION: frames/canon/MVI_5630_frame_0001.jpg — Slide showing Provocation #2 with card-based instructions -->

A card-based interpretation exercise structured in 4 rounds (~5 minutes per round):

1. Draw a card from the deck and hold it so that the illustration faces the group
2. Study the image together silently
3. Read through and react to the information on the back, beginning with "I suppose..." followed by what really happened
4. Pass the deck to the next person

This provocation trained participants in a skill essential to futures work: interpreting ambiguous visual signals and constructing meaning from incomplete information. The "I suppose..." framing explicitly separates assumption from reality — a critical distinction when working with uncertain futures. Each round built interpretive confidence and demonstrated how different people extract radically different meanings from identical stimuli.

### Provocation #3: Exquisite Futures

<!-- IMAGE SUGGESTION: frames/canon/MVI_5631_frame_0001.jpg — Slide showing Provocation #3 with round structure -->

Named after the Surrealist technique "Exquisite Corpse" — where artists collaboratively drew a figure without seeing each other's contributions — this provocation applied the same logic to futures speculation:

- **Round #1: 2035 / Global** (~7 minutes) — Tables collaboratively authored a vision of the global landscape in 2035, each participant contributing a piece without full visibility into others' contributions
- **Round #2: 2040 / Industry** (~7 minutes) — The same collaborative method, now focused on industry-specific futures in 2040

By splitting the exercise into a near-term global round and a further-out industry round, the provocation forced participants to bridge macro trends with sector-specific implications — the same bridging that effective foresight and business strategy require but rarely achieve in traditional planning processes.

### Provocation #4: The Spectacle — SXSW 2040

<!-- IMAGE SUGGESTION: frames/canon/MVI_5632_frame_0001.jpg — "The Spectacle: SXSW 2040" slide displayed to full room -->
<!-- IMAGE SUGGESTION: frames/canon/MVI_5632_frame_0002.jpg — Wide shot of packed room during Spectacle activity -->

The final provocation drew on the Situationist concept of "The Spectacle" — Guy Debord's critique of how mediated images replace lived experience. Tables were tasked with imagining what SXSW itself would look like in 2040, synthesizing the creative muscles exercised in the previous three provocations into a single collaborative vision.

This was the workshop's culminating act: participants who had spent the previous hour drawing, interpreting, passing, and co-authoring now applied all of those techniques to a shared subject they all understood — the festival they were attending. The specificity grounded the speculation, while the methods ensured the visions were genuinely surprising rather than incremental extrapolations.

## Methodology: Art Movements as Innovation Tools

The workshop's intellectual foundation draws from two avant-garde movements:

**Surrealism (1930s–1960s)** contributed techniques for bypassing rational thought to access unexpected ideas. The Surrealists believed that conscious, logical thinking was the enemy of genuine creativity — that the most powerful ideas emerge when the mind is freed from its habitual patterns. Techniques like automatic writing, exquisite corpse, and the "one into another" game were designed to produce exactly this cognitive liberation.

**Situationism (1950s–1970s)** contributed techniques for disrupting habitual behavior and perception. The Situationists — led by Guy Debord — argued that modern life had become a passive "spectacle" where people consumed images rather than creating meaning. Their response was to create "situations" — constructed encounters that broke routine and forced active participation. The "CHANGE PLACES" disruptions and the final Spectacle provocation both drew directly from this tradition.

The workshop's premise is that these techniques, developed by artists to disrupt cultural complacency, are directly applicable to disrupting strategic complacency in business. The same cognitive patterns that make daily life feel automatic also make strategy sessions produce predictable outputs. Artistic disruption methods break those patterns.

## Connection to SXSW 2026 Themes

This workshop embodied SXSW 2026's persistent tension between AI-driven efficiency and irreducibly human creativity. While dozens of sessions discussed how AI generates content faster, this session asked participants to generate ideas slower — to struggle, to draw badly, to interpret ambiguously, and to build meaning collaboratively without algorithmic assistance.

The workshop's methodology directly connects to several festival through-lines: the "Lose Your Mind to AI" session's warning about cognitive offloading (these exercises are cognitive fitness training), Ian Beacraft's call for organizations that think differently rather than just faster, and the Art and Creativity track's insistence that the creative process itself — not just its outputs — is what makes human work irreplaceable.

The Parallax Futures approach positions artistic disruption not as an alternative to strategic rigor, but as its necessary complement. In an era where AI can generate a hundred competent strategies in seconds, the competitive advantage belongs to organizations whose people can think in ways that surprise even themselves.
