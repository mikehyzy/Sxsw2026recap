# Craft Still Wins: Greg Greenberg Brought Apple's Creative Philosophy to SXSW 2026

## Overview

Greg Greenberg, Executive Creative Director at TBWA\Media Arts Lab, delivered one of SXSW 2026's most pointed counterarguments to the industry's AI fixation. In a solo Featured Session on March 14, 2026, Greenberg argued not against artificial intelligence but for the irreplaceable primacy of human instinct, taste, and emotional craft in creative work. Drawing on more than 14 years producing Apple's most iconic advertising, he used real campaign case studies to demonstrate that work resonating most deeply with audiences is not the most technologically sophisticated, but the most authentically human.

## The Session and Its Official Listing

The full official title was "Featured Session: Craft Still Wins: The Irreplaceable Role of Human Instinct, Taste, and Emotion," listed under event code PP1148516 in SXSW's Innovation Conference track. The SXSW app truncated it to "FEATURED SESSION: CRAFT STILL WINS: THE IRRE..."

The session was announced in December 2025 as part of the second wave of Featured Sessions for the Innovation Conference. SXSW's official description stated:

> "Have we lost sight that the creative work that truly impacts you on a human level, requires just that, the craft of a human? Greg Greenberg, award-winning Executive Creative Director at TBWA\Media Arts Lab, Apple's global creative agency, explores the irreplaceable role of human instinct, taste, and emotional intelligence in the creative process. Featuring real-world examples of handcrafted storytelling, he offers a rare perspective on how we can't lose sight of what truly impacts people."

Unlike the other Featured Sessions in the Innovation track, this session had no co-panelists or moderator. Greenberg presented solo, a format consistent with the session's framing as a singular creative perspective rather than a roundtable debate. The session was accessible to Platinum and Innovation badge holders as part of the Brand and Marketing programming strand.

## Greg Greenberg and 14 Years Shaping Apple's Advertising Identity

Greg Greenberg has spent more than 14 years at TBWA\Media Arts Lab, Apple's bespoke global creative agency, making him one of the longest-tenured senior creatives at an agency that is itself notoriously private. He has held the title of Executive Creative Director since the beginning of 2021, leading campaigns for iPhone and Apple Vision Pro.

His portfolio includes some of the most awarded Apple work of the past decade: the AirPods spot "Bounce," with its dynamic choreography and Framestore visual effects; the emotionally precise "Heartstrings" campaign; the cinematic metaphor of the Apple Vision Pro film "Submerged."

Before TBWA\Media Arts Lab, Greenberg worked at Goodby, Silverstein and Partners. His work has been recognized at D&AD, Cannes Lions, and The One Show. TBWA\Media Arts Lab's Shot on iPhone campaign, to which Greenberg contributed over many years, won the Grand Prix in the Creative Effectiveness Lions at Cannes, with jury president Andrea Diquez describing it as a masterclass in elevating user-generated content into a long-term platform with over 20 billion impressions.

The most revealing window into Greenberg's philosophy before SXSW came in a 2024 LBB profile and in his commentary on Apple's 2024 holiday campaign centered on the AirPods Pro 2 hearing aid feature. He described the creative process as beginning with a single arresting statistic: that people with hearing loss wait an average of 10 years before seeking help, and building entirely outward from that human truth.

"Authenticity was the driving force," he said. The production involved recording a sound designer's own brain using a binaural microphone and extensive consultation with Apple's hearing health team and the hard-of-hearing community. That campaign, and its reception, formed part of the case study material Greenberg brought to Austin.

## Human Instinct Versus AI Efficiency: The Case Greenberg Made at the Podium

Greenberg's central argument was not a rejection of AI tools but a defense of what he called the irreplaceable role of human emotional intelligence in creative decisions. He contended that the advertising industry risks conflating speed and scale with effectiveness, and that the campaigns that produce genuine cultural resonance originate in moments of human instinct that no algorithm can replicate.

He drew a contrast between Apple's crafted storytelling and the trajectory of established tech giants, arguing that the most dangerous creative failure is not making bad work, but making interchangeable work. His case studies from Apple's campaigns demonstrated how instinct and taste, specifically the willingness to slow down, to pursue handcrafted elements, and to prioritize emotional truth over executionalefficiency, translate directly into cultural resonance and brand equity.

A recurring theme was the behind-the-scenes investment in authenticity that audiences never see but always feel. Using the AirPods "Heartstrings" holiday campaign as an example, Greenberg described the casting process that required finding a real father with mild to moderate hearing loss who had a child who played guitar, and a family archive of iPhone videos that could be woven into the narrative. The specificity of that search, and the commitment to not casting an actor who could merely simulate the experience, was what gave the final film its veracity. The hard-of-hearing community's response confirmed it: the portrayal felt true-to-life in ways that a conventionally produced commercial would not have achieved.

Greenberg also addressed the collaboration question directly. Great creative work, he argued, does not emerge from one person's vision in isolation. It emerges from teams willing to apply extended scrutiny, to reject the first solution, and to hold a higher standard of what "done" means. That philosophy, cultivated at an agency that has signed legal agreements preventing its staff from discussing client work in public, produces a culture of craft that is structurally incompatible with the volume and velocity model that AI-assisted production enables.

## The Session in the Context of SXSW 2026's AI Debate

The session fit squarely within one of SXSW 2026's most visible fault lines: the tension between AI's operational advantages and the creative and reputational risks of what analysts called the "bland tax" that large language models impose on generic content.

Greenberg's session was programmed alongside and implicitly in dialogue with the CMO panel from Lyft and Samsung, which argued that AI supercharges rather than replaces authentic human creativity. Where those CMOs argued for AI as infrastructure, Greenberg argued for craft as the non-negotiable foundation that infrastructure must serve.

SXSW 2026, held March 12-18 in its 40th anniversary year, ran all programming — Innovation, Film & TV, and Music conferences — concurrently for the first time across seven days. Nearly one-third of PanelPicker proposals touched on AI, making it the most popular topic. The Brand and Marketing track, of which Greenberg's session was a part, accessible to Platinum and Innovation badge holders, ran all seven days and featured sessions from HubSpot CMO Kipp Bodnar, Mastercard CMO Raja Rajamannar, and futurist Amy Webb. In that landscape, a solo session from an advertising creative — one who was not a technology executive, an investor, or a futurist, but someone representing a discipline — represented a deliberate programming counterweight.

TBWA\Media Arts Lab itself used SXSW 2026 as a platform in prior years. The agency's global head of design, James Taylor, had previously presented at the conference on the role of design in advertising. Greenberg's selection as a Featured Session speaker, a designation reserved for the highest-profile programming, signaled both the industry's appetite for perspectives grounded in practice rather than prediction, and SXSW's deliberate effort to balance its AI-heavy agenda with voices arguing for the enduring value of human creative judgment.

## Conclusion

This session delivered a specific and evidence-backed argument in a week when the creative industry's professional class needed to hear it: that the demand for AI-generated content at scale does not eliminate the competitive advantage of doing one thing, slowly and with full human attention, exactly right. Greenberg's value as a speaker was precisely his operational credibility. He was not arguing for craft in the abstract. He was describing, with case-specific detail, what craft looks like in practice inside one of the world's most scrutinized creative relationships. The takeaway for marketing organizations was concrete: the investment in handcrafted work, in casting real people rather than actors, in consulting directly affected communities, in working with Academy Award-nominated directors rather than production templates, produces outcomes in audience trust and emotional response that cost-optimized, AI-assisted production cannot match. Whether that argument prevails commercially against the efficiency pressures documented elsewhere at SXSW remains the industry's central unresolved question.
