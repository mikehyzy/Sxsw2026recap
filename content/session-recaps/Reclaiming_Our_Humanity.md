# Reclaiming Our Humanity: The SXSW 2026 Session That Challenged AI's Power Brokers

## Session Metadata

**Date:** Sunday, March 15, 2026
**Time:** 4:00 PM - 5:00 PM CT
**Location:** JW Marriott Salon 6-8
**Format:** Featured Panel Discussion (Livestreamed)
**Track:** Tech & AI (SXSW 40th Anniversary)
**Moderator:** John Palfrey (President, MacArthur Foundation)
**Panelists:**
- Timnit Gebru (Founder & Executive Director, Distributed AI Research Institute)
- Karen Hao (Freelance Journalist, BBC Podcast Host, AI Spotlight Series Leader)

## Overview

On the afternoon of March 15, three of the most influential critics of concentrated AI power—Timnit Gebru, Karen Hao, and John Palfrey—shared a stage to dismantle the tech industry's dominant narratives and argue that neither AI utopia nor AI apocalypse serves the public interest. Named one of the most anticipated sessions of SXSW 2026's 40th anniversary edition, this featured session positioned itself as the festival's sharpest structural critique of an industry that dominated nearly every other conversation.

The central argument was deceptively simple but radical: the real question is not "What can AI do?" but "Who defines its purpose and for what goals?" By refusing both utopian ("AI will solve everything") and apocalyptic ("AI will destroy civilization") narratives, the panelists carved out a third position: AI as a domain of civic life where communities have rights, responsibilities, and avenues for collective action. The session systematically exposed what they called two "strategic distractions"—the utopia and apocalypse narratives—arguing that both serve to concentrate wealth and power within a handful of corporations while deflecting scrutiny from present, measurable harms.

## Key Themes and Insights

### The False Binary: Utopia vs. Apocalypse

The panelists opened by identifying what they termed the tech industry's masterful deployment of two "strategic distractions": the utopian narrative ("AI will usher in abundance and solve humanity's greatest problems") and the apocalyptic narrative ("AI will achieve artificial general intelligence and potentially destroy humanity"). Both narratives, despite their apparent opposition, serve identical functions: they concentrate attention on spectacular futures—either glorious or catastrophic—while deflecting scrutiny from present harms occurring right now.

This critique echoed a broader observation about how power operates: by making people choose between two extreme poles, the real questions about governance, equity, and accountability disappear from view. The panelists refused this trap, insisting that the conversation must return to the present: What is actually happening to real people, right now, as AI systems are deployed?

### The Invisible Human Labor Behind AI

A striking theme emerged: the invisibility of human labor in AI narratives. Thousands of workers globally label data under demanding conditions—repetitive work, tight deadlines, low wages, often in developing nations far from public view. These workers make AI possible, yet rarely appear in celebratory narratives about AI's capabilities. This invisibility is neither accidental nor neutral; it's structural. When AI appears as an autonomous intelligence emerging from algorithms, rather than as a system built on human labor extraction, the ethical dimensions disappear.

Karen Hao's investigative work has documented these dynamics across continents: data labelers in Kenya, water activists in Chile fighting data center consumption, environmental impacts in Arizona. Her reporting exposes what many AI narratives obscure: that artificial intelligence systems are built on foundations of human exploitation and environmental extraction.

### The Environmental Footprint of AI Infrastructure

Beyond labor, the panelists highlighted the massive environmental cost of AI development and deployment. Data centers consume staggering amounts of water and energy. As AI systems scale, these environmental impacts intensify. Yet environmental costs rarely appear in business models or policy discussions. This absence reflects power: those benefiting from AI have successfully externalized its environmental costs onto communities and ecosystems that see no benefit.

### How AI Might Stifle Cultural Evolution

A more subtle but profound concern emerged: AI trained on existing data may actively stifle cultural evolution by producing repetitive content rather than challenging social norms. Human art questions values and drives social change—it imagines different futures. AI systems trained on existing datasets tend to reproduce existing patterns and biases. If cultural production increasingly flows through AI systems trained on past data, the capacity for societies to imagine and build genuinely different futures may diminish.

### Specific Concerns About AI and Children

Gebru raised a specific concern about chatbots and AI systems being used by children and adolescents. These tools can generate dependency—young people becoming accustomed to AI as their primary source of validation, information, and companionship. The developmental implications are significant and largely unexamined. This worry parallels historical concerns about addictive technologies, but with added dimensions around cognitive development and social formation.

### The Hunuki Federation Model: Communities Controlling Their Own Data

Gebru highlighted the Hunuki Federation as an example of communities controlling their own data, goals, and deployment decisions. This model contrasts sharply with the current paradigm where data is extracted from communities and controlled by corporations. The Hunuki Federation demonstrates that alternative models exist—they're just not being scaled because they don't serve the interests of corporations seeking to maximize data extraction.

### John Palfrey and the MacArthur Foundation's Institutional Counter-Power

John Palfrey serves as the sixth president of the MacArthur Foundation, with approximately $8-9.2 billion in assets and more than $350 million in annual grants. His trajectory traces a direct line from internet governance pioneer to philanthropic architect of AI accountability. Under Palfrey's leadership, MacArthur has become arguably the most consequential philanthropic force in AI governance:

- **Humanity AI Initiative** (August 2025): A $500 million, five-year initiative co-launched with Omidyar Network and eight other major foundations (Ford, Mellon, Mozilla, Packard, Doris Duke, Lumina, Kapor, Siegel Family) targeting five priority areas: democracy, education, humanities and culture, labor and economy, and security

- **Aligned Grants**: $10 million in grants to organizations including the AI Now Institute ($2M), Data & Society ($500K), Brookings AI Initiative ($2M), and the Pulitzer Center ($1M)

- **Current AI Partnership** (February 2025): MacArthur joined as founding partner of Current AI, a global partnership targeting $2.5 billion for open, accountable AI development

Palfrey's guiding philosophy is declarative and actionable: "AI is not destiny, it is design." This framing rejects technological determinism and asserts that AI systems reflect human choices about values, priorities, and governance.

### Karen Hao's "Empire of AI" and the Power Consolidation Narrative

Karen Hao's research (documented in her book "Empire of AI: Dreams and Nightmares in Sam Altman's OpenAI," published May 2025 as an instant New York Times bestseller) maps how AI companies operate as modern empires: seizing resources, exploiting labor in the Global South, and projecting narratives of inevitable progress to cloak power consolidation.

Her investigative career demonstrates institutional credibility: she was the first journalist to embed inside OpenAI (August 2019), revealed how Facebook's Responsible AI team was structured to ignore the platform's most damaging algorithmic harms, and published explanations of AI bias cited by U.S. Congress and taught in universities. Her central critique is precise: "Silicon Valley, with OpenAI at its helm, has woven a remarkably compelling narrative about generative AI and AGI being the key to progress and abundance. This narrative cloaks what's happening beneath the surface."

### Timnit Gebru's "Many Models for Many Kinds of People"

Timnit Gebru delivered the session's most quoted line, capturing an alternative vision: "I don't want to build one model for everything. I want to build many models for many different kinds of people in the world, because there's no one way of being human."

This statement refutes the scaling-is-everything orthodoxy that drives current AI development. Gebru's work through the Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR), which she founded on the anniversary of her firing from Google, demonstrates that independent, community-rooted AI research is possible. DAIR operates with approximately 16 staff across three continents, funded initially by $3.7 million from Ford Foundation, MacArthur Foundation, Kapor Center, and Open Society Foundation.

Her recent projects include:
- **Data Workers' Inquiry**: A participatory research project spanning nine countries where data workers became community researchers, with testimony delivered at the European Parliament
- **Surveillance Watch**: An interactive map of the global surveillance industry
- **"Driven Down" Report**: On Amazon's algorithmic management of delivery drivers
- **Language Technology Projects**: For East African communities building NLP in their own languages
- **"The AI Con"** (published Harper, May 2025, co-authored with Alex Hanna): A critique of AI hype endorsed by Karen Hao
- **"The View from Somewhere"** (forthcoming fall 2026): Gebru's memoir/manifesto

### A Growing Ecosystem of Resistance and Alternative Development

The panelists are deeply embedded in a rapidly expanding ecosystem of organizations working to redistribute power over AI development:

**The AI Now Institute** (founded by Meredith Whittaker and Kate Crawford, now led by Executive Director Amba Kak) published its "Artificial Power: 2025 Landscape Report" in June 2025, mapping AI market power and offering strategies for community organizers.

**The Algorithmic Justice League** (founded by Joy Buolamwini) released its landmark "Comply To Fly?" report in July 2025—a two-year study of TSA facial recognition at 91 airports that found 99% of travelers received no verbal notice of their right to opt out.

**Data & Society** launched an "AI Civics" initiative in 2025, reframing AI literacy from individual skill-building to collective civic engagement.

**Connected by Data** (UK-based, led by Jeni Tennison) campaigns for collective data governance and co-organized the first Participatory AI Research and Practice Symposium at the Paris AI Action Summit.

**Labor Movements** have intensified engagement: the AFL-CIO launched its "Workers First Initiative on AI" in October 2025—the first comprehensive labor movement agenda on AI. The Tech Workers Coalition launched workersdecide.tech in December 2025.

**Global South Organizations** like Masakhane (2,000+ African researchers building NLP for 50+ African languages) and the Global Indigenous Data Alliance (which held a major conference in April 2025 with nearly 300 Indigenous delegates) are asserting data sovereignty against digital colonialism.

### The Interconnected Power Structure

The connections between the three panelists and this ecosystem are direct and significant:
- Palfrey's MacArthur Foundation funds DAIR, AI Now, Data & Society, and the Pulitzer Center
- Hao leads the Pulitzer Center's AI Spotlight Series and her reporting draws on DAIR's research
- Gebru's DAIR collaborates with many of these organizations

The throughline is strategic: shifting power over AI from a handful of Silicon Valley companies to communities, workers, and the public—through independent research (Gebru), investigative journalism (Hao), and philanthropic investment (Palfrey).

## Takeaways for Practitioners, Policymakers, and Citizens

### For AI Developers and Companies
- Recognize that narratives about AI inevitability serve power consolidation; be honest about trade-offs and limitations
- Make visible the human labor, environmental costs, and community impacts of AI development
- Engage genuine community participation in AI governance, not performative consultation
- Support independent research and criticism; view it as strengthening rather than threatening to responsible development

### For Policymakers and Regulators
- Reject false binaries (utopia vs. apocalypse) in favor of grounded, present-focused regulation
- Create accountability mechanisms for present harms: labor exploitation, environmental impact, bias, surveillance
- Support institutional alternatives to corporate-dominated AI development (DAIR, AI Now, etc.)
- Prioritize data sovereignty and community control over large-scale data extraction

### For Philanthropic and Academic Institutions
- Fund independent research on AI that critiques corporate narratives
- Support international, participatory research models (like DAIR's Data Workers' Inquiry)
- Create infrastructure for collective governance of AI systems
- Shift resources toward community-rooted, rather than corporate-led, AI research

### For Journalists and Media
- Investigate the actual conditions of AI development: who benefits, who bears costs
- Expose narratives of inevitability and automation hype as serving particular interests
- Platform research from independent institutes and Global South voices
- Follow the money: trace how AI investments flow and whose interests they serve

### For Workers, Communities, and Citizens
- Organize collectively around AI systems that affect your labor, data, and communities
- Demand transparency about how AI systems make decisions affecting you
- Support organizations building community power and data sovereignty
- Participate in collective governance models of AI systems

## Connection to SXSW 2026 Themes

This session crystallized SXSW 2026's overarching theme of "Humans" by refusing abstraction. While much of the festival debated AI's potential futures, this panel returned relentlessly to present reality: real workers labeling data under exploitation, real water being consumed by data centers, real children developing dependencies on AI systems, real power concentrating in a handful of corporations.

The session also embodied the festival's emphasis on agency and collective action. Rather than positioning humans as passive recipients of technological change, the panelists argued that humans—through research, journalism, organizing, philanthropy, and civic action—can shape AI's development and governance. This is not a utopian or apocalyptic claim, but a pragmatic assertion about power and its contestation.

The most distinctive contribution was the panelists' refusal to accept either pole of the dominant AI debate. By naming both utopia and apocalypse as "strategic distractions that serve to concentrate wealth and power," they carved out a third position: AI as a domain of civic life where communities have rights, responsibilities, and avenues for collective action. This position—neither technologically deterministic nor anti-technology—reflected SXSW 2026's mature approach to human-technology relationships.

Whether that third position can survive the gravitational pull of trillion-dollar AI investments remains the open question. But the infrastructure being built by DAIR, AI Now, the Algorithmic Justice League, Connected by Data, and the broader Humanity AI coalition suggests the counterweight is no longer theoretical. The alternative to concentrated AI power is not yet inevitable, but it is no longer impossible.
