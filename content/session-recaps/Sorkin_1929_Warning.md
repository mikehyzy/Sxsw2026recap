## Overview

Andrew Ross Sorkin used his marquee SXSW 2026 session to deliver a historically grounded warning to an audience steeped in AI optimism: the patterns driving today's technology-fueled speculation mirror the conditions that preceded the 1929 crash with uncomfortable precision. Interviewed by Bloomberg's Joe Weisenthal, Sorkin drew on his bestselling book *1929* to argue that Nvidia's dominance of the AI infrastructure trade, the democratization of risk through emerging financial instruments, and the conviction that "this time is different" mirror the conditions of a century ago—and that the guardrails preventing a repeat are being systematically dismantled.

The session landed at a charged moment: the S&P 500 had entered correction territory weeks earlier, Trump's tariff regime was roiling markets, and the AI bubble debate had reached fever pitch. SXSW's SVP of Programming framed the festival around "humans," and Sorkin's session was the sole feature event directly addressing financial crash history, giving it a distinctive role in the programming.

## Andrew Ross Sorkin: The Journalist Who Sees 1929 in the Mirror

### Career and Expertise

Andrew Ross Sorkin (born February 19, 1977) occupies a rare position at the intersection of financial journalism, television, and publishing. He:

- **Co-anchors CNBC's flagship morning program _SquawkBox_** (since 2011)
- **Founded the New York Times DealBook franchise** in 2001, establishing the foundation for modern financial journalism
- **Hosts the annual NYT DealBook Summit at Jazz at Lincoln Center**, which in 2025 featured Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent
- **Co-created Showtime's _Billions_** and won an Emmy in 2022 for Outstanding Live Interview

His first book, **_Too Big to Fail_** (Viking, 2009), chronicled the 2008 financial crisis from inside the boardrooms that nearly let the system collapse. It spent six months at #1 on the *Times* bestseller list, won the Gerald Loeb Award, and became an HBO film nominated for 11 Emmys.

### The 1929 Book and Media Tour

His second book, **_1929_**, published October 14, 2025 by Viking/Penguin Random House, applies the same immersive method to the greatest crash in Wall Street history. The product of **eight years of research** and roughly **600 pages** (including 100 pages of notes), it was named a Best Book of 2025 by The Washington Post, TIME, The Economist, Bloomberg, and Fast Company, and appeared on Barack Obama's Favorite Books of 2025 list.

The *Wall Street Journal* called it "one of the best narrative histories I've read," and Ron Chernow described it as "a timeless cautionary tale that speaks to the present no less than the past."

Sorkin's media tour for *1929* included:

- **60 Minutes** (October 12, 2025)
- **CNN, the Acquired podcast, the Hoover Institution's GoodFellows, and the All-In Podcast**

Across all outlets, he pressed the same thesis: the parallels are real, the timing is concerning, and the infrastructure that protected against 1929-style cascades is being dismantled.

## What Sorkin Told the SXSW Audience: Key Moments from March 15

The most detailed account of the session came from Brazilian journalist Thaís Monteiro of Meio e Mensagem, who attended in person and published a recap the same day, supplemented by the AI-generated session summary SXSW produced.

### On Nvidia and RCA: The Tech Stock Parallel

Sorkin compared Nvidia's dominance of the AI infrastructure trade to RCA's hold on the 1920s imagination. Both companies were described as "the future of technology" at moments of peak speculative enthusiasm, with valuations driven by narrative rather than current earnings.

The parallel is specific: RCA surged from $5.80 to $420 before crashing to $3—a company universally described as "the future of technology" that had never paid a dividend. Nvidia's current dominance mirrors this dynamic, with valuations driven by anticipated AI dominance rather than current financial performance.

### On AI as Double-Edged Sword

Sorkin described two failure modes. AI could create a classic speculative bubble that bursts when investment returns fail to materialize—hundreds of billions spent on chips and infrastructure with uncertain payoff. Alternatively, AI could succeed so well at automation that it destroys jobs and destabilizes the economy from the other direction.

Both paths carry systemic risk. Either way, the mathematics doesn't necessarily add up.

### On Celebrity Financiers and FOMO Psychology

The 1920s produced celebrities who preached that everyone should own stocks—John Raskob, a GM executive who pioneered consumer credit for car purchases, became a national figure arguing that ordinary people should own stocks. The psychology was FOMO (fear of missing out) dressed in populist language: "everyone should get on the train before it leaves the station."

Sorkin noted that the 2020s parallel is the pushes to open 401(k)s to private equity, venture capital, and crypto—wrapping risk in populist language. "Public companies were required to have disclosure rules. Private companies don't. But in the flag of democratizing finance, there's a lot of people who want access to that." The same pattern, applied to new asset classes.

### On Information Speed Then and Now

In 1929, stock prices arrived 4–5 hours delayed; investors had to physically travel to Wall Street to check whether they still had money. When the crash began on October 24 (Black Thursday), this information lag turned panic into paralysis.

Today's real-time markets move faster in both directions—amplifying both rallies and routs. Sorkin noted that information speed then and now creates different dynamics but equivalent risks.

### On Journalism's Role and the Public's Receptiveness

Sorkin argued that financial journalism exists to warn of imminent risks, as it did (ineffectively) in the 1920s. But the public rarely accepts warnings from media or specialists, preferring to take risks themselves.

The observation carried particular weight at a festival where media disruption was a recurring theme.

### On the Mood of the Moment: "We Seek Things to Worry About"

In one of the session's most quoted lines, Sorkin reflected: "I don't know if, as a society, we seek things to worry about. This moment, in terms of technology and geopolitics, leads us to that. In the 1920s, we were going through a technological revolution. People were excited. Today, it seems more complicated."

## Sorkin's Core Thesis: The Structural Parallels Are Real and Recurring

The book structures its narrative chronologically around specific dates from February 1929 through June 1933, anchored by a cast of larger-than-life characters. Its central figure is **Charles Edwin Mitchell**, chairman of National City Bank (now Citibank), who aggressively promoted stock speculation among ordinary Americans.

Other key players include:

- **Jesse Livermore**, the legendary short seller who made $100 million during the crash
- **William Crapo Durant**, GM's founder turned stock pool operator who lost everything
- **Roger Babson**, the economist ridiculed for predicting collapse
- **Carter Glass**, the senator who pushed the banking reforms that followed

### The Core Argument: Speculation Is the Twin of Innovation

Sorkin's epigraph, from Albert Einstein on October 26, 1929 (three days before Black Tuesday), captures the paradox:

> "No one, it seems, can benefit by the experiences of others."

His core thesis is that **pervasive optimism, unrelenting faith in rising prices, and the seductive power of new technology created conditions where collapse was inevitable—yet invisible to everyone living through it**. Yet he resists pure doomsaying. His essential message is not prediction but pattern recognition: **"speculation is the twin of innovation"** means bubbles are not merely failures but byproducts of genuine progress.

### The Guardrails Are Coming Down

Sorkin's most pointed claim concerns the regulatory architecture built after 1929:

- **The SEC was established** to enforce disclosure and prohibit manipulation
- **Stock pools were outlawed** to prevent coordinated price pumping
- **The accredited investor definition was created** to limit exposure to sophisticated investors
- **Prospectuses were required** for public securities

In 2026, each of these guardrails is being dismantled:

- The **CFPB is being defunded and dismantled**, SEC rules are relaxing, and the definition of accredited investor is being revised
- On 60 Minutes, Sorkin was direct: "It's not that we're going off a cliff tomorrow. It's that there's speculation in the market today, there's an increasing amount of debt, and all of that's happening against the backdrop of the guardrails coming off."

## Joe Weisenthal: The Ideal Interlocutor for a Crash Conversation

Joe Weisenthal (born September 2, 1980, Detroit) brought two decades of financial journalism and a UT Austin degree back to his home state to conduct this conversation. He is:

- **Co-host of Bloomberg's _Odd Lots_** podcast alongside Tracy Alloway—a twice-weekly show covering everything from the plumbing of repo markets to AI infrastructure spending
- **Executive Editor at Bloomberg**
- A figure steeped in financial history, having covered every market cycle since joining Business Insider in 2008

Weisenthal's career trajectory made him a natural fit for this conversation. He joined Bloomberg to cover the financial crisis in real time, rising to Executive Editor by 2013. He moved to Bloomberg in late 2014, eventually co-hosting the *What'd You Miss?* television show before focusing on the podcast and newsletters.

His recent work tracked almost precisely the themes Sorkin raised at SXSW. In November 2025, Odd Lots aired **"Why Paul Kedrosky Says AI Is Like Every Bubble All Rolled Into One,"** exploring how AI combines elements of real estate, tech, loose credit, and government backstop bubbles. In May 2025, he interviewed his former boss Henry Blodget about "what does history say about how big the AI boom can get, and when does a boom turn into a bubble?"

Weisenthal's media tour for *1929* pressed the same thesis across four outlets in late 2025 and early 2026, establishing him as both informed counterparty and genuine intellectual interlocutor.

## Conclusion: History as Framework, Not Forecast

Sorkin's SXSW appearance succeeded not as prophecy but as pattern recognition delivered at maximum relevance. His refusal to predict a specific crash—"I don't want to tell you that today is 1929. That, I have no idea about, and I think it's actually unlikely. But do I think we're in a bubble of some sort? Sure."—gave the argument flexibility and power.

The most lasting contribution may be the simple frame: **transformative technology and speculative excess are not opposites—they are twins**. Radio genuinely transformed the 1920s. AI genuinely transforms the 2020s. Both created conditions where speculation outran fundamentals.

For business leaders whose strategic planning assumes the AI investment continues indefinitely, Sorkin's session posed a necessary question: What happens when the math stops adding up? The session offered no answer—only history, pattern, and the insistence that "speculation is the twin of innovation" means every boom contains the seeds of its own unwinding.

At a festival where nearly every other session promised transformation, Sorkin offered something rarer: accountability to historical precedent.
