## Tom Sachs: The Artist

### Career and Practice

Tom Sachs (born July 26, 1966, New York City) is among the most distinctive figures in contemporary art—a sculptor, filmmaker, and self-described "professional hobbyist and bricoleur" who has spent 35 years building painstakingly handmade replicas of culturally powerful objects from plywood, duct tape, and found materials.

His studio, **Allied Cultural Prosthetics**, operates from the former machinery district of downtown Manhattan with a team of roughly 10–13 people working under a codified set of rules Sachs calls the **"Ten Bullets"**—a 21-minute instructional film (directed by Van Neistat, 2010) that functions as equal parts orientation video, code of conduct, and philosophical manifesto.

### Early Work and Trajectory

Educated at Bennington College and London's Architectural Association, Sachs got his start fabricating furniture in **Frank Gehry's Los Angeles shop**, where he adopted the organizational practice of "knolling" (arranging objects at parallel or 90-degree angles). After moving to New York around 1990, he created his breakthrough work: the **Hello Kitty Nativity** (1994), replacing the Virgin Mary with Hello Kitty—a piece that launched his career by fusing high art with consumer iconography.

His most iconic early works weaponized branding with surgical precision:

- **Chanel Guillotine (Breakfast Nook)** (1998)—fuses luxury fashion with revolutionary violence
- **Hermès Hand Grenade** (1995), **Tiffany Glock** (1995), **Prada Deathcamp** (1998)—all interrogate consumer desire through the language of luxury

### The Space Program Series

His magnum opus is the **Space Program series** (2007–present)—a cycle of five immersive exhibitions in which Sachs and his crew build full-scale replicas of spacecraft, mission control centers, and space suits from plywood, duct tape, and found materials, then perform complete space missions as though they were real. The series spans:

- **Missions to the Moon** (Gagosian, 2007)
- **Mars** (Park Avenue Armory, 2012)
- **Europa** (Yerba Buena Center for the Arts, 2016)
- **Rare Earths** (Deichtorhallen Hamburg, 2021)
- **Infinity** (Dongdaemun Design Plaza, Seoul, 2025—the largest exhibition of his career, featuring approximately 200 works)

His replica-making so impressed NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory that they commissioned him to design the actual EDL Mission Patch worn by JPL staff.

### Major Museum Holdings

Sachs's work is held by the **Metropolitan Museum of Art, MoMA, the Guggenheim, the Whitney, Centre Pompidou, LACMA, SFMOMA, the Smithsonian, and the Art Institute of Chicago**.

He is represented by **Gagosian, Acquavella Galleries, Thaddaeus Ropac, and Tomio Koyama Gallery**.

## The Philosophy: Sympathetic Magic and Belief as Creative Practice

### Origins in Frazer's Anthropology

The concept of "sympathetic magic" originates with Scottish anthropologist **Sir James George Frazer**, whose landmark 1890 work **The Golden Bough** identified two universal principles of magical thinking:

1. **Homeopathic or imitative magic** (Law of Similarity)—like produces like; a wax figure of an enemy can be used to harm them

2. **Contagious magic** (Law of Contact)—objects once in contact continue to influence each other; a lock of hair retains connection to the person it came from

Sachs has made sympathetic magic "a cornerstone of my work" and uses the term explicitly across decades of interviews, lectures, and writings.

### Application to Studio Practice

Sachs applies these principles literally. His replica-making operates on the principle that **the replica channels the power of the original**. When he builds a Nikon FM2 camera from plywood and duct tape, or a NASA spacesuit from found materials, the act of replication—the meticulous attention, the material fidelity, the labor investment—creates a magical connection to the original.

He told The Talks in a 2025 interview: **"Half of having an expensive camera is the image that it captures, but the other half is holding it and desiring it."** The implication: the replica captures the first half through faithful reproduction; desire and belief supply the second.

### The Daily Ritual: Output Before Input

Sachs's creative methodology is governed by a daily practice captured in one of the Ten Bullets: **"Work to Code: Creativity Is the Enemy"** (build incrementally on existing systems rather than improvising) and **"Output Before Input"** (create something before consuming media, email, or social platforms).

The practice: create something every morning before checking his phone—write, draw, touch clay, build. This daily output generates the meditative state where genuine accidents and mutations occur—"like genetic mutations in nature," as he told Interview Magazine in 2024.

His 2025 London exhibition **"A Good Shelf"** at Thaddaeus Ropac displayed 30 hand-formed ceramics—one new ceramic tea bowl created every morning before checking his phone. He credits David Lynch's **Catching the Big Fish** as a key influence and prepares the night before by setting out materials so the morning creation is frictionless.

### The Constraints as Creative Architecture

Sachs's material constraints—plywood, foam core, hot glue, duct tape, Sharpie, Benjamin Moore paint—are not limitations but creative architecture. He calls himself a **"black belt in bricolage,"** and his materials are deliberately limited, drawing from Claude Lévi-Strauss's work on bricolage: building with available materials rather than custom-engineered components.

The constraint forces innovation. When building a plywood Nikon camera, you cannot achieve the mechanical precision of the real thing, so you must achieve it through visual fidelity and emotional authenticity instead. The constraint transforms the work from imitation into reinterpretation.

## What Sachs Told SXSW: The 2026 Keynote

### The Opening Frame

Sachs opened the keynote by introducing himself not as a famous artist but as someone who has spent 35 years building painstakingly handmade replicas from humble materials. He used the term **"sympathetic magic"** explicitly, explaining Frazer's two principles and arguing that his entire practice is built on the conviction that **the act of making something is itself a form of magic**.

### The Key Argument: Belief as Creative Foundation

The keynote's subtitle—**"How to Get Other People to Believe in Your Vision By First Believing in Yourself"**—captures the argument. Sachs's position is that belief is not passive wishful thinking but an active labor-intensive practice. When you build something meticulously, with care, from humble materials, you are performing a ritual that manifests belief.

The corollary: belief is contagious. When an audience sees a full-scale plywood and duct-tape spacecraft and watches the artist perform a complete moon mission inside it as though it were real, something shifts in them. The doubt—"this is just plywood"—gives way to enchantment. They begin to believe because the artist believed first, and that belief made material.

### I.S.R.U. and the Collaborative Dimension

Sachs used the keynote to unveil **I.S.R.U.** (In-Situ Resource Utilization), described as his "latest conceptual project" and positioned as inviting the SXSW audience to participate.

I.S.R.U. appears to be a continuation of his Space Program series—asking audiences to imagine building a self-sustaining human settlement on another planet using only local materials. The project extends his collaborative practice (audiences have always been invited to participate in his space missions as though they were real astronauts) into a new register.

### The Philosophical Claim: Making as Spiritual Practice

Throughout the keynote, Sachs positioned making—the hands-on, material, labor-intensive act of building something—as a spiritual practice. Not in a religious sense, but in the sense that it connects the maker to something larger than themselves: the tradition of craft, the community of makers, the cultural power of objects.

He quoted extensively from his own interviews and writings, consistently returning to the idea that **work is the ritual. It is my sympathetic magic.** "I build these things because I want them to exist," he told the Brooklyn Rail. "I want them to exist" is both aesthetic choice and metaphysical claim: the act of building is an act of willing something into being.

## The Workplace Abuse Controversy and Its Aftermath

### The 2023 Crisis

In March 2023, **Curbed (New York Magazine)** published an exposé based on accounts from over a dozen former employees describing Sachs's studio as a "cult-like" environment characterized by:

- Verbal abuse, object-throwing, and screaming at employees
- Sexual comments about staff members' bodies
- Appearing on Zoom calls in his underwear
- A basement storage room called the "rape room" (later renamed the "consent room")

Employees described being told they were replaceable ("I don't care if she dies. There's a million of her and only one of me") and experiencing the "Ten Bullets" system as authoritarian control masked as discipline.

### The Response and Recovery

Nike initially distanced itself in May 2023, stating it was "not working with Tom's studio at this time." Sachs published a handwritten apology acknowledging he was "impulsive and even aggressive" while denying harassment. He committed to implementing external HR and mandatory harassment prevention training.

**However, in September 2024**, Nike resumed collaboration with Sachs, requiring "demonstrated recommitment to fostering a culture of respect and inclusivity." The art world's institutional infrastructure—Gagosian, Acquavella, Thaddaeus Ropac, Tomio Koyama—did not drop him. Galleries accelerated shows through 2024–2026, with the Seoul Infinity exhibition (his largest ever) opening in 2025.

### The SXSW Appearance as Rehabilitation Signal

Sachs's keynote slot—positioned within the cross-track Platinum programming tier, accessible to all badge holders—signals that SXSW and the broader cultural infrastructure have moved past the controversy. The fact that the session proceeded without public controversy or protest suggests either acceptance of his explanation and reforms, or institutional forgetting.

**A critical note:** No independent verification of the claimed studio reforms has been published, and no former employees have publicly confirmed improved conditions. The institutional acceptance appears to rest on Sachs's apology and Nike's conditional resumption of partnership, not on demonstrated change.

## The Tension at the Heart of the Session

The keynote's most striking feature is the tension between its philosophical message—that belief and making are sacred practices—and the 2023 exposé of a studio culture where the same "Ten Bullets" discipline was experienced by employees as authoritarian control.

The sessions invites audiences to believe in Sachs's vision. The 2023 allegations suggest that vision came at significant human cost to those implementing it.

Whether those two realities are reconcilable remains an open question. The SXSW appearance suggests the cultural infrastructure believes they are. Whether that belief is justified requires acknowledgment of the controversy and demonstration of genuine reform—neither of which appears in the available materials.

## Position Within SXSW 2026's Dominant Narratives

### Against Algorithmic Determinism

While most SXSW 2026 programming focused on how AI, biotech, and convergent forces would reshape society, Sachs offered a counternarrative: the act of making, believing, and building with your hands remains spiritually and culturally essential. The physical act of creation—plywood, duct tape, belief—can compete with algorithmic sophistication for cultural power.

### The Handmade in an Age of Optimization

SXSW 2026's broader arc moved from grand technological visions (Webb, Sorkin) toward human-centered practice (Bhargava, Serena Williams, FirstBuild). Sachs's keynote positioned the handmade—the meticulous, labor-intensive, deliberately imperfect act of building—as the antidote to optimization culture.

His materials (plywood, duct tape) are intentionally "wrong"—insufficient for the task, requiring ingenuity and care to function. This is the opposite of consumer culture's promise of perfect, frictionless experience. It is friction as virtue.

### Belief as Social Contagion

Bhargava argued that human connection is design, not accident. Sachs argued the same about belief and making—that when you build something meticulously, with care, others begin to believe in it. The two speakers occupy complementary positions: Bhargava at the interpersonal layer, Sachs at the material/maker layer.

## Most Notable and Surprising Elements

1. **Sympathetic magic as operating principle.** Positioning artistic practice through the lens of 19th-century anthropology—the idea that making something creates a magical connection to what is being made—is genuinely unusual and gave the keynote intellectual weight beyond typical creative practice talks.

2. **The constraint-as-creativity framework.** Sachs's insistence that plywood, foam core, and duct tape are features, not bugs—that constraints force innovation—inverts the culture's assumption that more resources = better outcomes.

3. **Output before input as daily discipline.** The practice of creating something every morning before checking email or social media captures a philosophy of prioritizing creative work over reactive consumption. Applied systematically, this generates the kind of prolific output (one ceramic per morning, for instance) that appears miraculous but is actually the result of relentless discipline.

4. **The unresolved tension with the 2023 controversy.** The keynote's position within SXSW 2026's "humans" theme makes the pre-existing allegations of workplace abuse contextually relevant in a way that isn't explicitly addressed. The tension between Sachs's philosophy of creative freedom and the experience of employees working under his Ten Bullets system remains unresolved.

## Conclusion: The Keynote as Cultural Moment

Tom Sachs at SXSW 2026 represents something more pointed than a celebrity creative talking about process. It's a statement about the enduring relevance of handmade, belief-driven, labor-intensive creative practice in an age of AI and algorithmic optimization.

The keynote's philosophical sophistication—grounding artistic practice in anthropological concepts of sympathetic magic, positioning belief as a contagious social phenomenon, arguing that constraints generate innovation—gave it intellectual weight that transcended the typical "here's how I make art" narrative.

The decision to program him at all, given the 2023 controversy, was itself notable. It signals either cultural forgetting or acceptance that the controversy was adequately addressed through apology and institutional reform.

Whether that belief is justified requires engaging with the unresolved tension: can Sachs's philosophy of creative freedom and disciplined making be separated from the workplace culture in which it was implemented? The keynote doesn't address that question. It simply positions belief and making as spiritual practices.

For an audience at SXSW 2026 steeped in AI talk, that message—that touching clay, building with plywood, and believing deeply in what you're making remain culturally essential—may have been genuinely countercultural.

Whether that belief is justified, and whether the maker whose philosophy inspired it has genuinely reformed, remains an open question.
