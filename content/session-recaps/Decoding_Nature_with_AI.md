# Decoding Nature with AI

## Session Metadata

**Date:** Monday, March 16, 2026
**Format:** Panel Discussion
**Track:** Science & Technology
**Duration:** 60 minutes
**Featured Speakers:** Glenn, Cesar, and Alicia (Basecamp, Microsoft, and academic researchers exploring AI applications in biology)

## Overview

This session brought together leading biologists and AI researchers to explore how artificial intelligence is transforming our understanding of the natural world—from discovering novel molecules to redesigning biological systems. The panelists discussed the paradigm shift happening in biological research: the move from traditional trial-and-error drug discovery to AI-driven "programming biology," where computational models predict molecular function and enable the design of custom therapeutics. Central to the discussion was the tension between unprecedented scientific opportunity and the ethical implications of increasingly powerful biological design tools.

The session revealed how data sovereignty, responsible AI alignment, and interdisciplinary dialogue between scientists and technologists are becoming essential frameworks for safely scaling AI applications in biology. Rather than framing AI as either savior or threat, the panelists emphasized that careful governance and ethical foresight must accompany scientific innovation. The conversation ranged from practical applications (gene therapy for rare diseases, antimicrobial resistance solutions) to systemic questions about data colonialism, regulatory frameworks, and the boundaries of what's ethically comfortable in biological design.

## Key Themes and Insights

### The Value of "Negative Data" in AI Training

Traditionally, biological research has treated failed experiments as mere footnotes—results not worth publishing. A fundamental insight emerged from this session: negative data (experiments that don't work) is invaluable for training robust AI models. When researchers can incorporate both positive and negative outcomes, models learn what works and what doesn't, creating more predictive power. This cultural shift in research practices—valuing unsuccessful experiments—reflects a broader change in how the scientific community thinks about AI-assisted discovery. In the words of one panelist, researchers now understand that negative data has become nearly as valuable as positive data for building effective predictive systems.

### Data Ownership and Benefit-Sharing in the Global South

Basecamp's approach to biological data collection across 31 countries demonstrates a real-world alternative to extractive data practices. Rather than parachuting researchers into developing nations to harvest biological data, Basecamp negotiates full access and benefit-sharing agreements from day one. Every country where they operate receives training, capacity building, local lab establishment, and long-term royalty streams from any commercialized discoveries. This precedent-setting approach directly addresses what critics describe as "digital colonialism"—the replication of colonial extraction patterns through modern data collection. By building these partnerships from scratch, Basecamp established data traceability, ownership clarity, and incentive alignment that create sustainable, equitable research infrastructure.

### Programming Biology: From Discovery to Design

The session introduced "programming biology" as a transformative paradigm that mirrors software engineering principles. Instead of discovering medicines through conventional drug trials spanning years, researchers can now:

1. Input a disease specification (e.g., hemophilia B)
2. Use AI to identify responsible genes
3. Design molecules computationally to correct the genetic defect
4. Validate computationally before animal testing
5. Dramatically accelerate to clinical trials

The example of Baby KJ—a child with a rare genetic condition whose custom-designed gene therapy was approved by the FDA within nine months—exemplified how AI can make rare disease cures economically viable by collapsing development timelines. What might have taken years of trial and error now happens through predictive modeling. This shift has profound implications for orphan drugs and conditions once deemed economically unviable to treat.

### Safety Benchmarking and Responsible AI in Biology

A critical safety framework emerged: AI models developed for biology must pass rigorous benchmarks before deployment. Standard practices include detecting jailbreaks through red-teaming, ensuring models won't generate bioweapons or dangerous pathogens, monitoring for misuse of generated sequences, and implementing comprehensive validation. These safeguards exist across leading labs (Open AI, Anthropic, Microsoft), but transparency, regulatory clarity, and standardization remain incomplete. The panelists emphasized that this infrastructure of safety is not peripheral to innovation but essential to its credibility and sustainability.

### The Dual-Use Challenge: Security Without Restriction

When AI can design antimicrobial molecules to fight drug-resistant infections affecting 5 million people annually, the same capability could theoretically create dangerous pathogens. Rather than proposing restrictions that might stifle beneficial research, the panelists advocated for conversation—genuine engagement with bioethicists, philosophers, public voices, regulatory bodies, and the scientific community itself. This dialogue helps communities define the boundaries of what they're "comfortable with" and builds shared ownership of responsible innovation.

### Scaling Infrastructure: Compute, Data, and Collaboration

Bringing biological AI to scale requires an unusual convergence: NVIDIA providing GPUs, cloud providers like Azure offering orchestration layers, and biotech companies supplying curated, ethically sourced datasets. This infrastructure discussion highlighted that "unsexy" technical infrastructure is as important as novel algorithms. The human genome project succeeded through massive collaboration across continents and institutions. Modern AI-driven biology requires similar coordination and interdependence, with each player bringing complementary capabilities.

## Takeaways for Practitioners

### For Life Sciences Leaders
- Invest in data infrastructure and governance from the start; retrofitting data ownership frameworks is costly and ethically complex
- Build safety benchmarking into development pipelines; red-teaming for biotech should be as rigorous as in other AI domains
- Engage ethical advisors, bioethicists, and community voices early—not as compliance theater, but as genuine strategic input
- Consider international benefit-sharing models not as philanthropic add-ons, but as sustainable business practices that build long-term research partnerships

### For AI Developers
- Biological AI isn't just about accuracy; responsible alignment with safety standards, transparency with regulators, and awareness of dual-use risks are table stakes
- Domain knowledge is non-negotiable; AI engineers in biotech must partner closely with domain experts, not merely apply general techniques
- Participate in red-teaming and adversarial testing to identify failure modes before systems are deployed

### For Policymakers
- The regulatory framework for AI-designed therapeutics exists (FDA approval pathways) but needs clarity and harmonization across jurisdictions
- Support international benefit-sharing agreements and data sovereignty frameworks that protect global equity and build trust in multinational research
- Create transparency requirements for AI use in drug development without creating barriers that consolidate research power among well-resourced entities

### For Researchers
- The economics of rare disease cures are changing; AI can make personalized medicine cost-effective by dramatically shortening development timelines
- Open dialogue about research boundaries and dual-use risks strengthens rather than weakens innovation
- Negative data has significant value; establish practices and infrastructure to capture and share unsuccessful experiments
