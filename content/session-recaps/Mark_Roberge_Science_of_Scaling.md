# Mark Roberge's Science of Scaling at SXSW 2026

## Overview

Mark Roberge — HubSpot's founding CRO, turned Harvard Business School professor and venture capitalist — delivered one of SXSW 2026's marquee Featured Sessions on March 17, presenting a data-driven framework that redefines product-market fit around customer retention rather than revenue. His session introduced the concept of "Leading Indicators of Retention" (LIR) as the north star metric for startups, drawn from his new book The Science of Scaling and a decade of teaching hundreds of founders at HBS.

The talk landed at a pivotal moment: SXSW's 40th anniversary, a conference dominated by AI hype, where Roberge offered a rare counterweight of rigorous, quantifiable startup methodology. His core argument — that 75% of funded startups fail because they scale haphazardly rather than scientifically — resonated deeply with attendees navigating the AI boom's frothy fundraising environment.

This was one of the first-wave Featured Sessions announced by SXSW in October 2025, placing Roberge alongside keynote-caliber speakers including Steven Spielberg, Amy Webb, Garry Tan (Y Combinator), and Jack Conte (Patreon).

## Who Is Mark Roberge: Engineer Turned Sales Scientist

Mark Roberge's credibility stems from an unusual trajectory: a mechanical engineer who became one of tech's most data-driven sales leaders, then codified what he learned into academic frameworks at Harvard. He holds a BS in Mechanical Engineering from Lehigh University and an MBA from MIT Sloan, where he sat next to future HubSpot co-founder Dharmesh Shah in class and was a semi-finalist in the 2005 MIT $50K Business Plan competition.

His career began at Accenture (1997–2000), where he worked on the team that wrote early code for UPS's tracking system. He then joined a mobile startup called Vettro (2000–2004) before founding PawSpot, a pet-sitting marketplace, during his MIT MBA — a venture that failed but forged his connection to HubSpot.

Roberge joined HubSpot in 2007 as the 4th employee and first-ever salesperson. Despite never having run a sales team, over the next seven years he scaled the company's annualized revenue from $0 to $100 million, grew the sales team from 1 to 450 people, and acquired over 10,000 customers across 60+ countries. He served as SVP of Worldwide Sales and Services (2007–2013), then Chief Revenue Officer of HubSpot's Sales Division through the company's October 2014 IPO on the NYSE (ticker: HUBS, priced at $25/share). He departed the executive team approximately a year after the IPO, transitioning to an advisory role.

Today Roberge wears three hats. He is a Senior Lecturer at Harvard Business School in the Entrepreneurial Management Unit, where he has taught for over a decade and authored 15+ HBS case studies on companies like Drift, InsightSquared, and ZenRecruit. He co-founded Stage 2 Capital in 2018 with Jay Po (former Bessemer Venture Partners investor), a venture fund now on its third fund at $150 million, investing in early-stage B2B software companies at approximately $1 million ARR. The firm has invested in 100+ startups across funds, with notable portfolio companies including Sendoso, Ocrolus, QuotaPath, DeepScribe, Arcade, Letter AI, Rally UXR, and Basis Theory. Exits include Momentum (acquired by Salesforce) and Metaplane (acquired by Datadog).

His 2015 bestseller The Sales Acceleration Formula was followed in February 2026 by The Science of Scaling: Using Data to Decide When — and How Fast — to Scale Revenue (Wiley, 256 pages), with 100% of proceeds donated to McLean Hospital for mental health research — a cause tied to his personal experience with PTSD following the September 11 attacks.

## The Frameworks: Retention as the True Measure of Product-Market Fit

The intellectual core of Roberge's SXSW presentation was a three-stage scaling framework with quantifiable milestones, anchored by a radical redefinition of product-market fit.

### Product-Market Fit Equals Retention, Not Revenue

Roberge explicitly rejects both Marc Andreessen's definition ("being in a good market with a product that can satisfy that market") as too subjective and Sean Ellis's 40% survey test as vulnerable to false positives. His position: "I just don't think sales have anything to do with product-market fit. You told the buyer a story, and they gave you some money. This has nothing to do with whether your product solves the problem or not."

His benchmark: annual logo retention above 90% and net dollar retention above 100% signal genuine product-market fit.

### Leading Indicators of Retention (LIR)

The problem is that retention is a lagging indicator — it takes quarters or a full year to know if today's customers will renew. This is where his signature innovation enters.

**Leading Indicators of Retention (LIR)** comprises the feedback loop. The formula is: "P% of customers achieve E event(s) within T time."

- P is the target percentage (typically 60–80%)
- E is an objective, instrumentable event representing the customer's "aha moment"
- T is the timeframe (usually 30–60 days for SaaS)

Famous examples include:
- Slack's benchmark of 70% of customers sending 2,000+ team messages in 30 days
- Dropbox's 85% uploading 1 file within 1 hour
- HubSpot's 80% using 5+ features within 60 days

Roberge prescribes tracking LIR via monthly acquisition cohorts — a chart he says should be the first slide in every board deck, ahead of the P&L.

### The Flywheel Concept

Roberge's go-to-market system is a self-reinforcing cycle, not a linear funnel. The Ideal Customer Profile (ICP) serves as the foundation, with demand generation and GTM hires as inputs. Pricing/packaging and compensation drive the internal mechanics, generating GTM activities, revenue forecasts, and revenue as outputs. Customer success data feeds back into ICP refinement, creating a virtuous loop.

This is philosophically aligned with but distinct from HubSpot's company-wide Attract-Engage-Delight flywheel (introduced by CEO Brian Halligan at INBOUND 2018). Roberge's version is specifically a GTM operating system for startups.

His ICP methodology uses a Green/Yellow/Red classification:
- Green customers — highest LTV and retention — are the only targets for outbound sales and marketing
- Yellow prospects are served if they arrive inbound but never proactively pursued
- Red prospects are disqualified immediately, even if they want to buy

Stage 2 Capital's data shows ICP-aligned customers have 24% shorter CAC payback periods and 425% higher expansion rate in the first 12 months.

## Three Stages and Five Failure Modes

Roberge's complete framework sequences startup growth into three stages, each with distinct hiring profiles and success metrics.

### Stage 1 (Product-Market Fit)

Focuses on consistently creating customer value. The north star is LIR achievement via cohort analysis. GTM philosophy: "do unscalable things" — founder-led selling, white-glove onboarding, personal networks. The right first sales hire is half product manager, half account executive, compensated on LIR achievement rather than contract signing.

### Stage 2 (Go-to-Market Fit)

Means generating customer success consistently and scalably. The north star shifts to unit economics: LTV/CAC greater than 3, payback under 12 months, Magic Number above 1.0. Roberge introduces "Leading Indicators of Unit Economics" (LIUEs) as short-term GTM activity goals extracted from long-term unit economics targets.

A critical warning: "Hiring salespeople without a scalable demand generation channel in place is a top 10 reason for Series A startup failure."

### Stage 3 (Growth & Moat)

Is about scaling revenue predictably while building sustainable competitive advantage. Scaling should be treated as a pace — "1/2 salespeople per month for 6 months, watch the speedometer" — not a lump-sum hiring event post-fundraise. The LIR chart and unit economics dashboard become the "speedometer"; if metrics break, slow down; if green, accelerate.

Roberge identifies five common reasons revenue acceleration fails: premature focus on top-line revenue over customer value, inadequate data-driven definitions of PMF, misunderstanding GTM capabilities needed before hiring salespeople, front-loading sales hires instead of pacing throughout the year, and confusing temporary competitive spikes with lasting advantage.

## Stage 2 Capital: Where Academic Theory Meets Venture Practice

Stage 2 Capital, co-founded by Roberge and Jay Po in September 2018, bills itself as "the first go-to-market venture capital firm." It invests in early-stage B2B software companies at approximately $1 million ARR, writing first checks of $4–6 million from Fund III ($150M).

The firm has invested in 100+ startups across funds, with a distinctive edge: an LP base of over 1,000 senior go-to-market executives — CROs, CMOs, and CCOs from companies like HubSpot, Snowflake, Atlassian, Zoom, Salesforce, OpenAI, and GitHub. These aren't passive investors; they're actively matched with portfolio companies as advisors, board members, and sometimes executive hires.

The investment team operates in "pods" pairing one partner with traditional VC experience and one with GTM operator background, ensuring every deal gets both financial and operational scrutiny.

Stage 2 also runs Catalyst, the first go-to-market accelerator — an 11-week program for seed-stage founders providing $100,000 investment plus hands-on tactical guidance on hiring scorecards, pricing models, sales playbooks, and marketing campaigns. The accelerator is a direct extension of Roberge's HBS curriculum into the venture context.

On the AI front, Stage 2 launched a Go-To-Market AI Lab that attracted 100+ CRO/CMO opt-ins and 200+ AI startup submissions within a week. The firm led Letter AI's $10.6 million Series A (AI-native sales enablement) and publishes GTM AI case studies biweekly.

Roberge's prediction: "We are about to witness the highest failure rate for a single cohort of startups in the history of tech" — referring to AI startups scaling prematurely without proven retention.

## From HubSpot's Playbook to Harvard's Classroom

Roberge's frameworks are not theoretical abstractions — they're battle-tested artifacts from HubSpot's scaling journey. His engineering mindset produced the Sales Hiring Formula, which replaced gut-feel recruiting with a scored rubric. He hypothesized traits, designed interview tests, scored every candidate, then ran regression analysis correlating scores with actual performance after six months.

The five traits that correlated most with HubSpot sales success were coachability, curiosity, intelligence, prior success, and work ethic — while traditional sales traits like aggressiveness and objection handling were negatively correlated.

His evolution of HubSpot's sales compensation through three stages — first rewarding customer acquisition, then customer retention, then sustainable growth economics — directly informed his current thinking on LIR and stage-appropriate metrics. This compensation evolution was published in Harvard Business Review in April 2015 and remains one of the most cited examples of data-driven sales management.

At HBS, Roberge teaches four courses: Entrepreneurial Sales and Marketing (second-year MBA elective), The Entrepreneurial Manager and Startup Bootcamp (first-year required courses), and Disrupting Silicon Valley with Artificial Intelligence — a field course taking ~50 MBA students to meet executives at NVIDIA, Waymo, OpenAI, Perplexity, and Meta. His January 2025 Silicon Valley trip produced several AI observations now woven into his frameworks, including that organizations will run on sequences of specialized AI agents, outcome-based pricing represents a major disruption opportunity for AI-native startups, and revenue per employee may become the critical north star metric.

## Conclusion

Roberge's SXSW 2026 appearance was less a conference talk and more a distillation of two decades spent at the intersection of startup operations, venture capital, and academic research. His central contribution — reframing product-market fit as a retention problem measurable through leading indicators rather than a revenue milestone celebrated prematurely — challenges the dominant venture-backed growth playbook.

The LIR framework gives founders something they rarely get: a quantifiable, real-time signal of whether their product actually works, available months before traditional retention data materializes.

What makes Roberge's perspective uniquely valuable in 2026 is its applicability to the AI startup wave. His warning about the coming "highest failure rate for a single cohort of startups in the history of tech" isn't pessimism — it's the logical conclusion of his framework applied to companies raising large rounds on demo-day hype without LIR-validated retention. The three-stage model (PMF → GTM Fit → Growth & Moat) provides a technology-agnostic but discipline-specific roadmap.

Whether you're building an AI agent or a traditional SaaS product, the question Roberge insists you answer first hasn't changed: are your customers actually succeeding, and can you prove it with data before you hire your next salesperson?
