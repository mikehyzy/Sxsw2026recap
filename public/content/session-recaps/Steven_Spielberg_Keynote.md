# Steven Spielberg Keynote: Wonder, Imagination, and the Filmmaker's Responsibility

**Speaker:** Steven Spielberg
**Interviewer:** Sean Fennessy, film critic and podcast host
**Date/Day:** Friday, March 13, 2026
**Format:** Keynote conversation and Q&A

## Overview

Steven Spielberg's keynote conversation at South by Southwest 2026 offered a rare, intimate look into the mind of cinema's most influential living filmmaker. At the conversation's heart lay Spielberg's core conviction that filmmaking is fundamentally about transmitting human emotion and connection—translating the "feeling you've been carrying around" into visual and emotional truth. Drawing from his childhood fears shaped by Disney's Fantasia, his fascination with close encounters and UFO phenomena, and his evolution from making intimate human dramas to increasingly pessimistic science fiction narratives, Spielberg reflected on how his art has been shaped by imagination, wonder, and an artist's responsibility to honestly explore the world as it is and might be. The discussion centered on his upcoming film Disclosure Day, a UFO thriller releasing June 12, 2026, marking his first extraterrestrial contact film in nearly 50 years since Close Encounters of the Third Kind (1977).

## Key Themes and Insights

### Imagination and Early Childhood Fears

Spielberg located the genesis of his creative impulse in what he calls an "abundance of fears"—childhood anxieties rooted not in external trauma but in an overdose of imagination. The Night on Bald Mountain sequence from Disney's Fantasia, viewed at age seven, paralyzed him with terror for over a year. Rather than inhibiting his creativity, this abundance of imagination became its engine. His response to fear was to create a "talisman to protect" himself—by scaring his sisters, he could externalize and control the fear. This impulse to transform internal emotional states into shareable experiences became the foundation of his filmmaking: taking feelings people carry but cannot fully articulate and making them visible and real on screen.

### Parental Influence and Media Censorship

Spielberg's parents exercised strict control over his media consumption, limiting him to shows like Jackie Gleason and Caesar's Show while forbidding him to watch the programs he actually wanted to see. This paradoxical denial of media—meant to protect but experienced as repression—paradoxically fueled his imagination. The forbidden nature of media he couldn't access made him imagine what those films and shows contained, creating narratives larger and more thrilling in his mind than any actual film could be. This experience shaped his understanding that "denial of the urge to live in a movie theater and not go to school" taught him that the greatest stories exist in the space between what you can see and what you imagine.

### First Film Experiences

The Greatest Show on Earth (1952) was Spielberg's first theatrical film experience at age six or seven. But the more formative early influence was Destination Moon, the first film to ground science fiction in realistic science. Destination Moon taught him that suspense could emerge from realistic detail—the specific challenges of reaching the moon, the contingencies of space travel—rather than from fantasy or melodrama. He asked his parents to buy him the soundtrack album (composed by Leith Stevens), his first record purchase, indicating how profoundly the film's scientific realism and aesthetic grounded in actual physics affected him.

### Close Encounters and the UFO Journey

Before Jaws' success in 1975, no studio would finance a UFO movie. Studios viewed UFO narratives as "fringe" and "crackpot" material. After Jaws opened the doors, Spielberg could finally make Close Encounters of the Third Kind—his dream film even before The Sugarland Express. The film represented the culmination of his lifelong fascination with extraterrestrial contact, influenced by consultant J. Allen Hynek, the former head of Project Blue Book who introduced Spielberg to people who had experienced close encounters of their own.

Spielberg reveals a startling personal fact: despite making multiple UFO films, he has never personally witnessed a UFO. "Half my friends have seen UFOs now called UAPs. I haven't." This admission highlights the distinction between artistic obsession and personal experience—Spielberg's lifelong fascination with contact narratives stems from intellectual and emotional curiosity rather than direct evidence.

### E.T. and Becoming a Parent

E.T. fundamentally shifted Spielberg's perspective on parenthood and family. The character's profound capacity for love—"his whole, it took over his whole body and it overwhelmed him"—inspired Spielberg to want children. Before E.T., he describes himself as "such an opportunist" focused entirely on storytelling without much personal life. The film made him "want to have children: 'I didn't want those kids to go home.'" The production choice to shoot in continuity for $10 million rather than in traditional economical sequences allowed child actors to develop naturally with their characters, creating genuine emotional resonance.

Spielberg maintains a close relationship with Drew Barrymore to this day, texting her "right until last week," reflecting how profoundly the experience of making E.T. bonded him to the child actors and to the film's themes of parental love and connection.

### Evolution of Science Fiction Storytelling

Spielberg acknowledges a significant tonal shift in his science fiction films—from the warmth of Close Encounters and E.T. to the suspicion and dread of AI, War of the Worlds, and Ready Player One. This shift reflects his subconscious response to an increasingly troubled world. He notes that "the subconscious rubs off on every choice," meaning that his growing awareness of global anxieties inevitably influences his artistic output, even when those choices aren't consciously calculated.

War of the Worlds was specifically made as a metaphor for 9/11, which "affected me in a very profound way." The choice came from recognizing that 9/11 represented a real-world analog to alien invasion—sudden, catastrophic, and fundamentally disorienting. Interestingly, Spielberg distinguishes between his earlier optimistic UFO narratives and his contemporary skepticism: the shift reflects not cynicism but increasing awareness of "the world as it is, currently and as I hope it could be someday."

### Current Views on UFOs and Extraterrestrial Contact

A 2017 New York Times article by Helene Cooper, Rasal Thal, and Leslie Keen about Navy pilot David Fravor's encounter off the USS Nimitz rekindled Spielberg's interest in UFO phenomena. The article's seriousness—treating the subject with journalistic rigor rather than sensationalism—combined with 2023 congressional subcommittee hearings featuring testimony under oath from intelligence and military personnel, further legitimized the phenomenon in Spielberg's mind.

Yet Spielberg emphasizes: "I am not afraid of any aliens." He remains open to the possibility that "we are not alone here on Earth right now. Maybe even today." His long-held belief that "we were not alone" in the universe coexists with scientific skepticism about whether contact is imminent.

### Disclosure Day and Contemporary Filmmaking

Disclosure Day represents Spielberg's first UFO film in nearly 50 years. The film addresses "the social dislocation that could occur" if alien interaction were announced, while explicitly rejecting the idea that such disclosure would be "a lethal disruption at all." The film acknowledges theological and social implications without succumbing to apocalyptic sensationalism. This measured approach reflects Spielberg's mature understanding that the most profound stories are those that take real anxieties seriously without exploiting them.

### Relationship to History and Storytelling

Spielberg is "drawn like a magnet to history," having excelled in history as the only subject at which he was "any good at in high school." His father was a science fiction reader who also loved reading biographies, bringing him into the importance of reading about history and the Holocaust or the Civil War. This dual inheritance—father's interest in both speculative and historical narratives—shaped Spielberg's core conviction that "I find so much richness in stories about the past." He finds contemporary stories challenging precisely because he can't locate them in historical context with the same richness and resonance.

Yet Disclosure Day and Minority Report represent rare ventures into contemporary and near-future storytelling, suggesting that Spielberg is consciously working to address contemporary concerns despite his natural gravitational pull toward history.

### Filmmaking as Emotional Translation

Throughout the conversation, Spielberg emphasizes his core belief: filmmaking is about finding feelings you've been carrying—maybe something you can't name—and putting it on screen so others recognize themselves in that feeling. This explains his return to UFO narratives not as a believer or skeptic but as an artist interested in the specific anxiety of not knowing whether we are alone, the hope embedded in seeking contact, and the potential transformation implied by confirmation of extraterrestrial intelligence.

## Takeaways for Practitioners

1. **Emotion Over Explanation:** Spielberg's approach prioritizes emotional truth over logical explanation or plot mechanics. In storytelling, the feeling a viewer carries away matters more than the coherence of plot details. Focus on translating human emotion into visual and narrative form.

2. **Imagination as Resource, Not Obstacle:** Childhood fears and overactive imagination, often seen as liabilities, can become creative assets. Create space for imaginative exploration rather than seeking to eliminate it through overstimulation or distraction.

3. **Constraints Enable Creativity:** Spielberg's parents' media restrictions paradoxically enhanced his imagination by forcing him to imagine what he couldn't see. Limitations, used thoughtfully, can drive more resourceful and imaginative solutions than unlimited resources.

4. **Study History and Science:** Deep knowledge of historical and scientific detail grounds contemporary storytelling. The specificity of Destination Moon's realistic space physics made it more suspenseful than fantasy alternatives. Learn the rules of your domain deeply.

5. **Subconscious Honesty:** Your subconscious will "rub off on every choice" you make. Rather than fighting this, acknowledge it. Spielberg's shift toward darker science fiction narratives reflects honest engagement with contemporary anxieties rather than conscious messaging.

6. **Long-Term Artistic Evolution:** Successful artistic careers aren't defined by single works but by honest engagement over decades with evolving concerns. Spielberg's return to UFO narratives at age 79 represents not redundancy but maturation—bringing greater nuance and restraint to lifelong themes.

7. **Maintain Genuine Curiosity:** Spielberg's UFO fascination persists without requiring personal experience or certainty. Sustain artistic engagement with genuine questions—not answers you've already reached.

8. **Child Actors and Continuity:** When working with young performers, shooting in chronological continuity allows natural development, creating more authentic emotional performances than traditional economical scheduling.

## Connection to SXSW 2026 Themes

Spielberg's keynote aligns profoundly with SXSW 2026's emphasis on authentic innovation and human connection:

**Wonder and Discovery:** Spielberg's career is built on rekindling wonder—the sense that the world contains mysteries worth exploring and that human connection transcends rational explanation. This resonates with SXSW's core values of discovery and celebration of the unexpected.

**Responsibility and Truth-Telling:** Rather than exploiting anxieties about extraterrestrial contact or technological change, Spielberg insists on treating these subjects with seriousness and honesty. For SXSW's creative community, this models the responsibility that comes with cultural influence.

**Evolution Over Repetition:** Spielberg doesn't rest on historical achievements (Jaws, E.T., Schindler's List) but continues to evolve his artistic voice in response to changing circumstances. This growth mindset—staying engaged with contemporary concerns while deepening historical knowledge—reflects SXSW's values of continuous innovation.

**Human Emotion as Central:** In a festival increasingly focused on technology, data, and disruption, Spielberg's insistence that filmmaking is fundamentally about recognizing and transmitting human feeling serves as a necessary corrective. The most impactful creative work connects to something true about human experience.
